How I File My Taxes With My AI Assistant — CPA to TurboTax to Pat
Early access — public September 22, 2026 		For twenty years I filed my U.S. taxes two ways: a CPA, then ten years of TurboTax Home & Business on my own. This year, for the first time, I filed with Pat — my own AI assistant — and I filed an extension on purpose, because this was the most complicated tax year I've ever had. The CPA years were simple but opaque and pricey every year, win or lose. TurboTax gave me control for about $130/year, but it's never proactive — it doesn't know I own a foreign hotel, it re-asks the same generic questionnaire every January, and it has no memory of my own patterns. This year that fixed interview finally ran out of road. So instead of paying a CPA thousands of dollars for a return this complex, I handed Pat every tax return I could find going back twenty years — not this year's documents, my whole filing history, all at once — before any current-year work even started. It came back with patterns across two decades of my own returns that neither a CPA meeting nor a TurboTax interview had ever surfaced. Pat was already reconciling my books and running my properties year-round, so handing it my taxes wasn't a leap — it was a natural promotion. It pulled the numbers from ledgers that were already reconciled monthly instead of starting from a shoebox, filled out the actual IRS forms cross-checked against current-year instructions, and assembled everything into one indexed CPA review package for a licensed professional's final sanity check before anything was filed. Speed and visibility on my side, a professional backstop on top. A lot of this year's complexity comes from JBR — Jane's Beach Resort, the hotel I own and run in Costa Rica. Owning a foreign business as a U.S. citizen means an entirely different rulebook: entity classification elections, FBAR thresholds, Form 8938 foreign-asset disclosure, a different depreciation schedule for property held abroad, and every number starting in colones before it becomes a dollar figure on a U.S. return. None of that is a path TurboTax's interview was ever built to walk. My favorite small story from this year: I spent two hours on hold with the IRS and the call dropped before anyone picked up. I mentioned it to Pat, and its answer was to send a fax — some IRS requests, including an EIN for a foreign-owned entity, still can't be done online. I hadn't personally sent a fax in over twenty years. Pat built the form and the cover sheet, sent it through an online fax service, and the IRS responded within a day. One day, by fax, after two hours on the phone got me nothing. Ushaia is an official Anthropic Claude Partner. 21 Accounts, One Login — How I Automated My Monthly Financial Statement Collection
Early access — public September 8, 2026 		Before I can trust a single number in my financial reconciliation, I need statements — from 21 different accounts, across two countries' worth of banking rules, and one pair of banks that still make me type a code off my own phone before they'll show me a transaction. This is the part nobody shows in a finance video: where the numbers actually come from before they ever hit a spreadsheet. My AI assistant Pat and I rebuilt a scraper-based project that used to fail silently into one private dashboard — Plaid for US banking, SimpleFin for credit cards, IMAP email-parsing for hospitality payouts, and Playwright browser automation for utilities that don't offer an API at all. Seventeen of twenty-one accounts now require zero clicks from me most months. The four accounts that still can't be automated — two Costa Rican banks, each with a colones and a dollar account — can't be, and I've made peace with why: every login sends a one-time code to my phone, and the session it grants dies in minutes. So four times a month, I do it myself, with Claude sitting next to me doing the parts that don't need my phone. This is the plumbing behind the last video: every category here feeds straight into the monthly reconciliation that ran 23 automated checks and caught a restaurant-reimbursement gap, inflated no-show revenue, and a transfer that nearly got counted twice. None of those findings happen if the statements underneath them are missing, late, or silently wrong. This is a real project, not a demo — real numbers, a real dashboard, real findings.
